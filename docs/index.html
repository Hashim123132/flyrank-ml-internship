Abstract

Question: FlyRank's content-review problem is triage at scale - which of thousands of published pages to open first - so the question here is whether a team, using only signals observed before the evaluation window, can be pointed at the pages most likely to go down before they go down.

Data: we rank 57,117 live pages from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse (41 anonymized clients, 79M rows, queried via DuckDB over Hugging Face), using observed signals only - no future windows, no label-derived fields, no IDs as features.

Method: a transparent CTR-gap rule as baseline, plus a random forest and logistic regression on observed signals; both scored on a client-grouped holdout and re-audited on a later, time-aware holdout with strictly pre-label-window features.

Result: the random forest reaches P@20 0.70 (AUC 0.574) on the same split with a 0.57 base rate; the time-arm audit sits at chance (AUC 0.522). Same-window triage works; the lead-time claim does not.

What it's for: a ranked, tiered review queue (P1/P2/P3) that tells an editor which pages to open first - decision support that keeps a human in the loop, never automation of the edit itself.

Introduction - the editor's hour

This is the FlyRank content-refresh problem: customers publish pages continuously, performance drifts, and a weekly review decides which pages are refreshed first. In this case study - the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse queried via DuckDB over Hugging Face - an editor has one hour and a list of 57,117 pages. Which pages should they open first? Without a score, "first" means whatever sorts to the top of a spreadsheet - and in our weak-pick study that is exactly how the habit rule lost: many of its top-20 picks were not currently down.

The cost of a wrong call is asymmetric. A missed declining page keeps losing CTR for weeks; a false pick burns one reviewer hour - cheap, but it adds up when the queue is long. This project builds a ranked queue so the hour lands on the pages where decline is most likely and most expensive.

The question is deliberately narrow: same-window triage, not prediction months ahead. We will show that the honest lead-time claim fails its own audit - and that the same-window claim survives it.

Data - what we used, and what we left out

Source: FlyRank ML Internship warehouse via DuckDB on Hugging Face (fact_content_query_90d + dim_content) - a gated, one-row-per-page snapshot, no client names or raw queries anywhere. Warehouse total: 79M+ rows, hundreds of clients. Analysis slice after exclusions: 57,117 rows, 41 clients.

Excluded, with reasons:

  • Rows with impressions_90d < 500 or no real position (avg_position_90d = 0) - below the volume floor the CTR signal is mostly noise.
  • impressions_last30 / impressions_prev30 as inputs - they are the label source; evaluation only.
  • IDs (content_hash_id, client_hash_id) as features - grouping only, never predictors.
  • The 90-day aggregates in the time-aware model - they overlap the label period.
  • No product flags exist in the warehouse (no health score, priority score, or action type columns to mis-use).

Public-safety: everything here is pseudonymous category labels and anonymized IDs; no client-identifying detail appears anywhere in this paper or the repo.

Methodology - label, baseline, model, validation

Label (one sentence)

A page is down when its last-30-day impressions are below its previous-30-day impressions - measured on the same data as everything else, used only for evaluation, never as an input.

Baseline - the transparent rule (Week 4)

A rule an editor can verify by hand: score = (position ≤ 20) × gap-to-tier-median-CTR × log(1 + impressions). It flags 25,167 pages of 57,117 (44%). It is the fair comparison because it is measured on the same data, same split, same metrics as the model.

Model - random forest + logistic regression on observed signals (Week 5)

Ten features, all observable at ranking time: log-transformed volumes (impressions, clicks, word count, search volume), raw observed fields (CTR, position, content age, competition), two missing-data flags, and five position-tier dummies. Missing keyword/word-count is flagged, not imputed as a real value.

Validation design

  • Client-grouped split: 33 train clients / 8 holdout clients, so pages from the same client never straddle the split. Test slice: 13,709 pages, base rate 0.570.
  • Leakage checks (all verified in the notebooks): rule inputs disjoint from label sources; features disjoint from the label and from IDs; a time-arm audit trains only on strictly pre-label-window features and scores on the label - the honest lead-time measurement.

What the errors look like - the honest part:

  • On the deep tier (rank 51+, 1,106 pages, down rate 0.75), the model has limited skill: the playbook deliberately excludes these pages from priority tiers.
  • The time-arm audit - strictly pre-label-window features only - measures AUC 0.522 (base 0.630): at chance. Same-window ranking works; the lead-time claim does not.

Limitations & honest framing

  • No lead-time claim survives the time-arm audit. Re-scored on strictly pre-label-window features, both approaches land at chance (AUC 0.522, base 0.630). The measured value is same-window triage, not prediction "before it happens".
  • Deep-tier pages are unrankable here (1,106 pages at rank 51+, down rate 0.75) - excluded from tiers by design; the signal there is noise-adjacent.
  • Base rate keeps P@K honest but not flattering: 0.57 of the test slice is down, so P@20 = 0.80 is +0.23 over random. The lift is real but modest; AUC is the number to quote.
  • Leading-indicator guesses leak into the queue: some top picks may not be currently down - fine as a flag, unsafe as an action; the checklist requires a SERP check first.
  • One export, one point in time: seasonality and refresh cycles are under-explored; these numbers are observed on this cohort, directional for others.
  • No causal claims at all: we rank what is correlated with decline; we never ran an experiment.

Ranked recommendations - the action playbook

The queue sorts all 57,117 pages and assigns every pick a tier, an archetype, and a human action:

TierPagesActionWhen
P1 - review first6,308review_firstHigh-volume pages whose CTR gap vs their own tier is wide and whose decay risk is top-ranked. Open page + SERP, then update.
P2 - refresh plan11,260refresh_planVisible, aging pages with a measured gap. Plan a refresh in the next cycle.
P3 - monitor only7,599monitor_onlyBelow the action floor. Watch one more window; never burn a reviewer hour here.
not tiered31,950no_actionNo signal above the floor.

The human checklist before acting on any pick - 1) page text matches the query it ranks for; 2) the SERP intent fits the page (sub-intent mismatch explains many gaps); 3) tracker consistency between last and previous windows (a tracker break mimics a huge gap); 4) recent edit/warm-up history (young pages ramp, not decay); 5) only then decide: adjust text, plan a refresh, or watch one more window.

The no-go list (never automate):

  • Never auto-edit titles, metas, or body text - editing without the SERP check produces harm.
  • Never auto-delete or deindex pages; the queue is review-only.
  • Never act on a single deep-tier page (rank 51+).
  • Never treat the P@K numbers as a promise of future hits - the audit shows at-chance on future-safe inputs.
  • Never feed the queue trend columns, IDs, product flags, or the label.
  • Never reuse this queue on a later export without re-running the notebook.
